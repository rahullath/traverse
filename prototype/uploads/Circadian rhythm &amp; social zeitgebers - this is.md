## Circadian rhythms and social zeitgebers

Circadian rhythms are generated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), which synchronises primarily to light–dark cycles but is also modulated by “zeitgebers” (time-givers) like meal times, activity, and social contact. Social zeitgeber theory (Ehlers, Frank, Kupfer) argues that disruptions to regular social routines destabilise circadian and other biological rhythms and can precipitate mood episodes, especially in depression and bipolar disorder. Empirically, social schedules (work times, classes, family routines) measurably shift sleep–wake patterns, and this impact is moderated by chronotype (morning/evening type).[^3][^7][^1]

For your chain model, any stable external event (lecture time, shift start, regular call with a friend) is a social zeitgeber that can be repurposed as the “anchor” from which you build the chain, instead of abstract clock time. This frames the app as helping the user re-weave a disrupted social-biological rhythm, not as optimizing their “productivity” for capital.[^7][^3]

## Implementation intentions and keystone actions

Implementation intentions are if–then plans: “If situation X occurs, then I will do behaviour Y.” Thirty years of work (Gollwitzer and colleagues) shows that these plans substantially increase goal completion across domains because they automate cue detection and response, reducing reliance on willpower and executive control. Neurocognitively, they seem to prime perceptual and attentional systems so the specified cue is more likely to trigger the planned response even under cognitive load.[^2][^8]

“WHEN I wake up, I shower” is literally a single implementation intention that also acts as a behavioural zeitgeber: it stabilises the transition from sleep state to active state and cascades into downstream behaviours (getting dressed, leaving the house). Your chain view becomes “one or two if–then links anchored to reliable cues” rather than endless to-do items; the app only needs to help the user articulate these if–then bindings clearly enough to internalise them.[^1][^3]

## Behavioural activation: act first, mood follows

Behavioural activation (BA) comes from Lewinsohn’s behavioural theory of depression: when people become depressed, they withdraw from reinforcing activities and contexts, which further reduces positive reinforcement and deepens depression. BA is deliberately non-insight-focused; it treats mood as an outcome of patterns of behaviour and environmental contingencies, and its core move is scheduling specific, value-based actions and having the person do them even when they feel nothing.[^9][^4][^10]

Modern BA protocols structure activities by value domains (relationships, work/study, recreation, health, etc.) and target avoidance patterns, not “productivity deficits.” Your chain, if it’s built from small, concrete, value-aligned steps (open curtains, text one friend, eat something, walk to bus stop), is a micro-BA schedule that doesn’t require the user to believe any motivational story first. That fits your politics: it locates the “fix” in reorganising one’s environment and micro-actions, not in moralising the individual as lazy or undisciplined.[^4][^10][^9]

***

## Temptation bundling without dopamine traps

“Temptation bundling” (Milkman) explicitly pairs a guilty-pleasure activity with a more effortful “should” behaviour: you only get the indulgence while or after doing the hard thing. In experiments, giving people access to addictive audio novels only at the gym increased workout frequency versus controls, and participants were willing to pay for such devices, implying genuine value rather than pure manipulation.[^11][^12][^6]

This is different from the dopamine micro-interventions baked into infinite scroll and streak systems, which rely on intermittent rewards, novelty, and social comparison to keep you trapped in the app. In your design, temptation bundling can be made explicit and user-authored: “While I’m in the shower I’ll listen to my favourite track,” or “I only get my fancy coffee on days I walk to campus,” with the app doing nothing beyond helping them script and externalise that contract.[^6][^11]

## ADHD time blindness as temporal myopia

Russell Barkley describes ADHD not as a time-management issue but as “temporal myopia”: nearsightedness with respect to the future. Time-blind people experience time as “now vs not-now,” underestimate how long tasks take, struggle to sequence actions toward future goals, and often cannot “feel” future consequences enough for them to guide present choices. Research links these difficulties to executive function differences and, possibly, to cerebellar contributions to time perception, not to laziness or lack of care.[^13][^5][^14]

Your backwards-from-anchor logic (start from an external, non-negotiable event and build a short chain of concrete steps leading up to it) is an evidence-congruent way to scaffold a damaged sense of temporal distance. It reduces the need to internally represent “60 minutes” and instead asks, “What absolutely has to be true just before this thing? And just before that?” which is much easier for a time-blind brain.[^5][^14]

## Design principles for a stateless, anti-retention tool

Translating this into an app that refuses neoliberal self-optimization:

- Stateless by design
    - No accounts, no cloud storage, no history, no streaks.
    - Each session is ephemeral: the user generates a plan, screenshots it or writes it down, and the app forgets.[^4][^5]
- Single-screen, short sessions
    - One main screen per session: “pick one anchor → build one chain → leave.”
    - Hard time limit (e.g., auto-close suggestion or fade to “Go live this, not tap here”) after a couple of minutes to block rabbit-holing.[^14][^6]
- No gamification, no self-quantification
    - No points, leaderboards, progress graphs, or productivity scores.
    - Language focused on “making the day more habitable” rather than “maximising output.”[^6][^4]
- Value language, not capitalist metrics
    - BA-style domains: “body,” “connections,” “study/work,” “care tasks,” “play,” surfaced as equal, not subordinated to paid labour.[^10][^4]
    - Copy that validates rest and limits instead of glorifying hustle.

## Concrete flows you can implement

### 1. Anchor + chain builder (zeitgeber + BA + implementation intention)

Session flow:

1. Ask: “What’s one external anchor in the next 2–8 hours?”
Examples: “lecture at 3pm,” “flatmate gets home,” “cat’s dinner,” “sunset,” “alarm tomorrow morning.”[^3][^7]
2. Ask: “What state do you want to be in at that moment (minimum viable state)?”
Examples: “washed and dressed,” “have eaten once,” “emotionally braced, not in bed,” “already outside.”[^9][^4]
3. Work backwards as a constrained chain (max 3–4 links):
    - “To be in that state, what absolutely needs to happen just before it?”
    - Each answer becomes a chain step with a rough duration slider (1–5 minutes, 5–15, 15–30) purely to force realism; you don’t store results.[^5][^14]
    - Keep steps BA-consistent: observable, small, specific (“put bowl of food for cat,” “put clothes on chair,” “open curtains”) rather than vague (“get my life together”).[^9][^4]
4. Translate chain into one or two explicit implementation intentions:
    - “When [anchor] happens, I will already have [last step done].”
    - You can also offer “When I [cue], I’ll immediately [first step].”[^8][^2]
5. Output as a plain text block meant to be copied out of the app:
    - Prompts: “Screenshot this or write it on a sticky note. The app won’t remember it for you.”
    - Then show a big button: “Close app and go do the first step.”[^4][^5]

No metrics, no checkboxes, no data retention; the app is effectively a structured scratchpad for shaping one chain while the user’s executive function is available.

***

### 2. Social zeitgeber planner

Instead of clock times, let users specify anchors as social or environmental events:

- “After my first WhatsApp message in the morning, I will open the curtains.”
- “When my flatmate starts making tea, I will stand up and stretch.”
- “When I feed the cat, I will drink a glass of water.”[^7][^1][^3]

Mechanically, this is just an if–then sentence template library with example cue types (social, bodily, environmental, digital) and a picker for tiny actions. You’re exploiting the fact that these events are naturally salient and rhythmically stable, so the brain needs less internal time tracking.[^2][^8][^1][^3][^5]

***

### 3. Explicit temptation bundling

A simple modal:

1. “What’s one thing you genuinely want to do today that feels a bit indulgent?”
Example categories: media, food, games, online browsing.[^11][^6]
2. “What’s one thing you feel dread about but know matters?”
Example categories: admin, uni work, cleaning, basic care.[^10][^4]
3. Offer two bundle structures as radio buttons:
    - “I will only [indulgence] while [task].”
    - “I will [task] first, then [indulgence] immediately after.”[^12][^11]

Again, you output a single sentence contract and encourage the user to externalise it (note, message to friend) and close the app. It’s temptation bundling as self-authored BA, not as a dark pattern to hold attention.[^11][^6]

***

### 4. Time-blind-friendly interface decisions

To respect ADHD time blindness:

- Use concrete ranges, not exact times
    - “Sometime between lunch and 3pm” instead of demanding 14:10.[^14][^5]
    - “This chain is for the next ‘chunk’ of the day” (morning/afternoon/evening).
- Emphasise sequence over duration
    - UI shows vertical stacks: “Now → Next → After that → Anchor,” mirroring your chain logic.[^5][^14]
    - You can add an optional “too many steps?” warning if the user exceeds 4 items, nudging toward compassionately minimalist plans.
- Normalise time-blindness psychoeducation
    - One small, static info panel: “If you struggle to feel time, that’s a known ADHD trait called time blindness / temporal myopia; it’s about how your brain senses time, not a character flaw.”[^13][^14][^5]
    - No suggestion that the goal is to become a perfect clock; the goal is to make the next few hours lighter.

***

## Patterns that resist neoliberal capture

To keep the app from collapsing back into a self-optimization product:

- Refuse to rank activities by economic value; a shower, a walk, and replying to a friend are “first-class citizens” alongside coursework.[^10][^9][^4]
- Avoid integrations that tie directly into wage-labour infrastructures (no Jira, Outlook, or KPI dashboards).
- Make offboarding a success metric: e.g., “If you’ve used this for a few weeks and can sketch chains on paper, delete the app. That means it worked.”[^4][^5]

From a scientific perspective, what you’re building is a thin, user-controlled interface to: circadian and social entrainment (zeitgebers), automatized if–then planning (implementation intentions), value-based activation (BA), reward coupling (temptation bundling), and temporal scaffolding for ADHD time blindness. From a political perspective, it’s deliberately refusing the usual surveillance, gamification, and productivity tropes and instead helping the user internalise a few simple structures and then leave.[^1][^2][^3][^6][^14][^5][^4]
